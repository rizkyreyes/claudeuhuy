Steel is almost eight times denser than water (structural steel ~7,850 kg/m3 vs fresh water 1,000 kg/m3; Gard: ~8,000 kg/m3, "about eight times")
  - https://gard.no/insights/why-do-ships-float/
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steel (density 7,750-8,050 kg/m3)
A solid steel block sinks; a hull of the same steel floats because the whole ship (steel + air inside) has a lower average density than water
  - https://gard.no/en/insights/why-do-ships-float
  - https://cursa.app/en/article/why-steel-ships-float-buoyancy-and-archimedes-principle-explained
Archimedes' principle: the upward (buoyant) force equals the weight of the water the object pushes aside (displaces)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedes%27_principle
  - https://gard.no/insights/why-do-ships-float/
A ship sinks until the water it displaces weighs as much as the ship; a ship's weight is called its displacement
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_(ship) ("the weight of water that a ship displaces when it is floating, which in turn is the weight of the ship")
  - https://gard.no/insights/why-do-ships-float/
Loading cargo makes the ship sit deeper (draft increases with displacement)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_(ship)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draft_(hull)
A load line (Plimsoll line) painted on the hull shows the deepest a ship may be loaded safely; marks TF, F, T, S, W, WNA for water density and season
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_line_(watercraft)
  - https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/web/20201218022555/https://oceanservice.noaa.gov/facts/plimsoll-line.html (NOAA: What is a Plimsoll line?)
  - https://maritime-executive.com/article/fast-facts-what-is-a-plimsoll-line
A ship floats deeper in fresh water than in salt water, because fresh water is less dense (fresh-water marks sit above the summer mark)
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Load_line_(watercraft)
  - https://merchantnavydecoded.com/what-is-international-load-line-and-draft-marks/
An empty ship rides high, part of the propeller comes out of the water; ships take seawater into ballast tanks (low in the hull) to sit deeper and stay stable
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_ballast (sailing "in ballast": water added to increase propeller immersion, steering, trim and draft)
  - https://www.marineinsight.com/a-guide-to-ballast-tanks-on-ships/
  - https://maritimepage.com/ballast-system-cargo-ship/
Ships are divided by watertight bulkheads into compartments, so a breach floods only part of the hull
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulkhead_(partition)
  - https://www.marineinsight.com/a-guide-to-ballast-tanks-on-ships/
Titanic could stay afloat with four (forward) compartments flooded; the iceberg opened more than four (Britannica: at least 5; other analyses: 6)
  - https://www.titanicinquiry.org/BOTInq/BOTReport/botRepObserv.php (British Wreck Commissioner's Inquiry: afloat with the four forward compartments flooded, not with No. 6 boiler room too)
  - https://britannica.com/question/Why-did-the-Titanic-sink ("could reportedly stay afloat if as many as 4 of its 16 compartments were breached ... affected at least 5")
