nuclear-power-plant (3d-21), facts checked 10 Nov 2026 (cloud run, search excerpts; energy.gov and nrc.gov fetches are blocked in the cloud network).
Every claim has an official source (DOE, NRC, EIA) or two agreeing sources. Wording kept general where sources differ.
Cut / not claimed: oil or gas equivalents per pellet (DOE says 120 gallons of oil, NEI 149, EIA ~150), an exact pellet size in mm, the exact
number of rods per assembly (264 in a 17x17 design, but DOE only says "more than 200"), how fast a scram stops the reaction, the exact
reactor count (EIA 62 of 93 PWRs in 2023, DOE "more than 65%"), how long cooling water must flow after shutdown, plant-specific numbers.

A nuclear power plant boils water: the heat from fission boils water into steam that spins a turbine ("giant kettle" is our analogy)
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ("Nuclear power reactors use heat produced during atomic fission to boil water and produce pressurized steam")
  - https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/nuclear/nuclear-power-plants.php
If the cooling water stops flowing the core can melt; Three Mile Island Unit 2 partially melted down on 28 March 1979 after the core lost coolant
  - https://www.nrc.gov/reading-rm/doc-collections/fact-sheets/3mile-isle.html (NRC backgrounder: TMI-2 partial meltdown, equipment malfunctions, design problems and worker errors)
  - https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/partial-meltdown-at-three-mile-island ("caused the core to lose water coolant")
Fuel = uranium processed into small ceramic pellets, stacked into sealed metal tubes called fuel rods; more than 200 rods bundled into a fuel assembly
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
A pellet is about the size of a gummy bear (DOE poster: "A one-inch uranium pellet, about the size of a gummy bear"); WNA: not much larger than a sugar cube
  - https://www.energy.gov/documents/nuclear-science-week-poster
  - https://world-nuclear.org/nuclear-essentials/how-does-a-nuclear-reactor-work
One pellet holds about as much energy as a ton of coal (NEI: one pellet = one ton of coal; DOE poster: same energy as one ton of coal; whatisnuclear.com calc ~1.3 t, so "about")
  - https://www.nei.org/fundamentals/nuclear-fuel
  - https://www.energy.gov/documents/nuclear-science-week-poster
  - https://whatisnuclear.com/calcs/energy-equivalents-of-one-fuel-pellet.html
In the core uranium atoms split (fission), releasing heat
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
Control rods are inserted or withdrawn to control the chain reaction; they absorb neutrons, so inserting them slows or stops it
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ("Control rods are inserted or withdrawn to control the rate of the nuclear chain reaction")
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scram ("control rods absorb neutrons so as to slow or stop the chain reaction")
About two-thirds of US reactors are pressurized water reactors (DOE: more than 65%; EIA: 62 of 93 operating reactors in July 2023)
  - https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/nuclear/nuclear-power-plants-types-of-reactors.php
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work
In a PWR the water around the core reaches about 600 °F (~315 °C) but does not boil, because the primary loop is kept at about 2,250 psi (~155 bar) by the pressurizer
  - https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressurized_water_reactor ("usually around 155 bar (15.5 MPa 153 atm, 2,250 psi)"; coolant ~315 °C)
  - https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1427/ML14274A090.pdf (NRC Reactor Concepts Manual, PWR systems)
The hot primary water flows through tubes (U-tubes) in the steam generator and boils a separate secondary loop of water into steam; the tubes are the barrier between the two loops
  - https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/power/pwrs.html
  - https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1427/ML14274A090.pdf
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ("the steam ... goes through a secondary, intermediate heat exchanger to convert another loop of water to steam, which drives the turbine")
The steam drives the turbine, the turbine shaft drives the generator -> electricity
  - https://www.nrc.gov/reactors/power/pwrs.html ("high and low pressure turbines are mounted on a common shaft that drives the main generator")
Then the steam is condensed back into water in the condenser and pumped back to the steam generator
  - https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1427/ML14274A090.pdf ("The resulting water is pumped out of the condenser ... and pumped back to the steam generator")
  - https://www.energy.gov/ne/articles/nuclear-101-how-does-nuclear-reactor-work ("Once through the turbine, the steam cools back into water, which can then be used over again")
The cloud from the big cooling towers is water vapor; the cooling water is a separate (third) loop that never touches the reactor water
  - https://cdp.dhs.gov/shared/se/courses/default/AWR-923-W%2005122021%201.2-20210512144644/groups/243.html (DHS: "visible as water vapor ... cooling towers are part of a nonradioactive system")
  - https://cwnuclear.com/sites/default/files/Download-Library/Nuclear-Myth-Nuclear-Cooling-Towers-Emit-Smoke.pdf
  - https://www.nrc.gov/docs/ML1427/ML14274A090.pdf (three loops in most PWRs: primary, secondary, condenser cooling / cooling tower)
