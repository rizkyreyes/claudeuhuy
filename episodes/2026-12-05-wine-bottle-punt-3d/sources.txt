1. The dent is called a punt.
   - Wine Spectator, Dr. Vinny, "Why is there an indentation in the bottom of a wine bottle?" https://www.winespectator.com/articles/why-is-there-an-indentation-in-the-bottom-of-a-wine-bottle-46387
   - VinePair https://vinepair.com/wine-blog/why-wine-bottles-have-punts-bottom/ ; Mental Floss https://www.mentalfloss.com/food/alcohol/reason-why-wine-bottles-have-dents-bottom
2. Hand-blown bottles: the base came out with a seam / a sharp point of glass in the middle; glassblowers pushed it up into the bottle so it stands level and no sharp point sits underneath. -> Wine Spectator (above); VinePair (pushing the seam up helped the bottle stand steady); Mental Floss; The Takeout https://www.thetakeout.com/2065005/why-wine-bottle-have-bottom-dent-punt/
   - "A bottle like that wobbles" = the standing-level point of the same sources (a point under the base cannot stand level).
3. The dome may have made the base stronger. -> Wine Spectator ("may have added strength"); Decanter, "Does a wine bottle punt mean better quality?" https://www.decanter.com/learn/advice/wine-bottle-punt-ask-decanter-330517/ ("believed to be originally crafted to help stabilise the bottle ... and to strengthen its structure"). Kept as "may have". Wikipedia (wine bottle) notes there is no consensus explanation -> on-screen note "SOURCES DIFFER ON THIS".
4. Machine-made bottles are tougher; for most (still) wine the punt is mostly tradition; some bottles have no punt. -> Wine Spectator (machine-made bottles stronger, mostly tradition except sparkling); search excerpt of the same set: "When machinery replaced glassblowers ... the punt remained as a nod to tradition. Some flute-shaped bottles are flat-bottomed."
5. Sparkling wine: the punt helps spread out / withstand the internal pressure; a flat bottom would be a weak point. -> Wine Spectator (sparkling: spreads out the pressure); Decanter (strengthen, especially for sparkling wines); glassofbubbly.com "The Anatomy of a Sparkling Wine Bottle" https://glassofbubbly.com/?p=94709 (punt adds strength to the base, the weakest point under pressure).
6. Pressure in a sealed champagne bottle ~5-6 atmospheres (~70-90 psi). -> BBC Science Focus https://www.sciencefocus.com/science/how-much-pressure-is-there-in-a-champagne-bottle ; VinePair https://vinepair.com/articles/champagne-bottles-so-heavy/ (five to six atmospheres, 70-90 psi); Wine Spectator https://www.winespectator.com/articles/does-the-size-of-a-champagne-bottle-make-a-difference-in-the-pressure-inside-47211
7. About three times the pressure in a car tire. -> Cosmos Magazine https://cosmosmagazine.com/physics/bubbles-the-physics-of-champagne ("about three times that found inside a car tyre"); Live Science https://www.livescience.com/5988-champagne-corks-dangerous.html ; Wine Spectator (tire about a third of champagne's level). Car tire ~30-35 psi vs ~90 psi.
8. Champagne bottles use much thicker, heavier glass. -> VinePair "Why are champagne bottles so heavy" (above; thicker and heavier, still-wine glass would fail at that pressure). Gram figures (800-900 g vs 400-500 g) NOT used: only one excerpt.
9. A deeper dent does not mean better wine; it can be a bit of a gimmick. -> Wine Spectator (punt depth says nothing about quality, "can be a bit of a gimmick"); Gear Patrol https://www.gearpatrol.com/home/a720199/further-details-wine-bottle-indent/ (no real relation between punt depth and quality); Decanter (above).
10. The sediment-trap idea has not been proven. -> Wine Spectator (sediment explanation unproven); Culinary Lore (sediment explanation makes little sense).

Cut / not used: the pontil (punty) rod detail (sources differ on how the dent was made), riddling, "looks fuller", bottle gram weights, any exact burst-pressure number.
