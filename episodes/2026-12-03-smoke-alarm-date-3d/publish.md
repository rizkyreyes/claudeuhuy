## YouTube description
How It's Built #43
Every smoke alarm has an expiration date: NFPA 72 says replace it 10 years after the date of manufacture printed on the back, not the date you put it up. Inside is a small dark chamber. In a photoelectric alarm an LED shines across it, aimed away from a light sensor; smoke scatters the light onto the sensor and the horn sounds. Ionization alarms use a tiny sealed speck of americium-241 to ionize the air between two charged plates so a weak current flows; smoke particles grab the ions, the current drops, and the alarm sounds. Over the years dust, insects, grease and damp air get into the chamber and the sensor slowly loses sensitivity, no matter how often you test it. The study behind the 10-year rule expected roughly 3 in 10 alarms to fail by then (CPSC). NFPA: almost 3 in 5 U.S. home fire deaths (2014-2018) happened with no smoke alarm or one that didn't work. Read the date; if it's over 10 years, replace the whole alarm. Simplified illustration; generic alarm, no brand.
