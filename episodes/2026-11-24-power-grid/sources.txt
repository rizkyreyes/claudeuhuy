Sources: power-grid (3d-31), checked 18 Nov 2026 (cloud run, web search excerpts).

"traveled here at hundreds of thousands of volts" / "up to seven hundred sixty-five thousand volts" / generator "at a much lower voltage"
  -> DOE CESER Electricity Grid Backgrounder (2023): plants generate at low voltages (5-34.5 kV); step-up substations raise it; typical transmission voltages 115, 138, 230, 345, 500 and 765 kV
     https://www.energy.gov/sites/default/files/2023-11/FINAL_CESER%20Electricity%20Grid%20Backgrounder_508.pdf
  -> EIA "How electricity is delivered to consumers": transformers step voltage up for long-distance transmission and down for distribution
     https://www.eia.gov/energyexplained/electricity/delivery-to-consumers.php
  (Not every outlet's power rode a 765 kV line; "hundreds of thousands" = the 115-765 kV transmission range. 765 kV is said as the top value only.)
"Let that into your house, and the wires in your walls would fail"
  -> home branch wiring is 120/240 V service (sources below); NM cable is rated 600 V (NEC / UL 719), a tiny fraction of 115,000+ V. Kept general, no number spoken.
"its voltage changes at least three times on the way" (step-up at the plant, step-down at the substation, step-down at the pole)
  -> DOE backgrounder (step-up and step-down substations, distribution below 34 kV) + DOE archived EERE "electricity grid" page (transformers on distribution lines lower it to 120/240 V)
     https://webarchive.library.unt.edu/eot2008/20090118060600/http://www.eere.energy.gov/de/electricity_grid.html
  ("at least": some routes add sub-transmission steps.)
"Inside, two copper coils wrap one iron core. The output coil has more turns, so the voltage climbs"
  -> basic transformer principle (voltage ratio = turns ratio): Wikipedia "Transformer"; EIA delivery page. Video shows a single-phase cutaway; real step-up units are usually three-phase (not claimed otherwise). Oil-filled tank shown (mineral-oil-immersed transformers: ERMCO pole-mount spec sheet below).
"Same power, higher voltage, means less current. And wires waste energy as heat, by the square of the current. Double the voltage, and that heat drops to a quarter."
  -> P = V x I and resistive loss = I^2 x R (standard physics): EEPower "Principles of high voltage transmission" https://eepower.com/technical-articles/principles-of-high-voltage-transmission/
     NASA GSFC "Why high voltage" https://pwg.gsfc.nasa.gov/Electric/-E11-reason.htm
"Now the power can cross hundreds of miles"
  -> EIA delivery page (long-distance high-voltage transmission lines); TANC "Understanding transmission" https://www.tanc.us/understanding-transmission/the-transmission-system-and-how-it-works/
"The wires up there are bare. Aluminum, around a steel core. Air is the insulation, and insulator discs keep it off the towers."
  -> Wikipedia "Overhead power line": most common transmission conductor is ACSR (aluminum conductor steel-reinforced); most of the insulation is provided by air; lines above ~69 kV use suspension strings of porcelain, glass or polymer discs, more discs at higher voltage
     https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhead_power_line
"Near town, a substation steps it down to under thirty-four thousand volts"
  -> DOE backgrounder: step-down substations; distribution systems typically rated below 34 kV
"the gray can on the pole ... two hundred forty volts, split into two halves of one hundred twenty. Your outlet gets one half. Your dryer gets both."
  -> Wikipedia "Distribution transformer" (pole-mounted, center-tapped 120/240 V split-phase secondary): https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_transformer
  -> Maddox "Low voltage distribution systems": https://www.maddox.com/resources/articles/low-voltage-distribution-systems
  -> ERMCO single-phase pole-mount transformer spec (oil-immersed, 120/240 V secondaries): https://www.ermco-eci.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/SPhPlMnt-Final.pdf
  (each hot leg is 120 V to the grounded center-tap neutral, 240 V between the two hots: dryers/ranges use 240 V)
"Only about five percent is lost on the whole trip"
  -> EIA FAQ "How much electricity is lost in electricity transmission and distribution in the United States?": about 5% of electricity transmitted and distributed, annual average 2018-2022
     https://www.eia.gov/tools/faqs/faq.php?id=105&t=3

Cut / not claimed: 60 Hz and real-time grid balancing (kept out for length), number of US grids, specific generator voltage, span lengths (video spaces towers 75 m apart for the frame), conductor sizes, the number of discs per voltage (sources vary).
