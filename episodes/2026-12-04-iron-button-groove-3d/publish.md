## YouTube description
How It's Built #45
The little notch at the tip of many irons is a button groove: a shallow slot along the front edge of the soleplate, between the hot plate and the body. Slide the tip in beside a button and the button settles into the gap, out of the heat, while the plate presses the fabric right up to its edge. Run a hot plate straight over a plastic button and it can melt, crack or get burn marks, so iron around buttons, not over them. Inside most irons, the heating element is cast into the aluminum soleplate, and a bimetal strip thermostat bends as it heats, cutting the power, then reconnects as it cools. A valve drips water from the tank into a steam chamber in the hot plate, where it flashes into steam and leaves through the holes. Too cold, and the water leaks out instead, which is why many irons have an anti-drip valve. Hard water leaves limescale that clogs the steam holes. Many irons switch off by themselves when left face down, often after about 30 seconds (times vary by model: check your manual). Simplified illustration: generic iron, groove drawn deeper than real.
