## YouTube description
How It's Built #48
A circuit breaker protects the wiring in your walls, not the things you plug in. Most home breakers have two trip mechanisms. Overload: the current runs through a bimetal strip; when too much is plugged into one circuit, the strip heats up, its two metals expand at different rates, it bends, pushes the latch, and a spring snaps the contacts apart. Short circuit (hot touching neutral or ground): thousands of amps rush in, a small coil becomes an electromagnet and yanks a plunger that trips the latch instantly. The arc between the parting contacts is pushed into the arc chute, a stack of metal plates that splits and cools it until it goes out. Most tripped breakers stop in the middle: push the handle to OFF, then back ON. Breakers are trip-free, so holding the handle on doesn't stop a trip. If a breaker keeps tripping, never replace it with a bigger one: the wire stays the same size and can overheat first. Find the cause or call a licensed electrician. Fire figure: NFPA, about 32,620 US home fires a year from electrical distribution and lighting equipment (2015-2019 average). Simplified illustration: one generic thermal-magnetic breaker drawn bigger, parts arranged for a clear side view; real layouts vary by maker.
